## Purpose

After finite-element discretization, a weak PDE is no longer an infinite-dimensional variational problem. It becomes a finite-dimensional system whose unknowns are coefficient vectors and whose equations are represented by matrices or matrix-like operator actions. Optimization adds more of the same numerical work: metric inversions, Newton corrections, adjoint solves, equality-constrained KKT systems, and active-set subproblems all eventually ask us to solve linear systems.

The phrase **solve a linear system** can hide a great deal. The matrix may be sparse or never assembled at all. It may be symmetric positive definite, nonsymmetric, singular, or symmetric indefinite. A direct factorization may be appropriate for a small problem but impossible to store for a large one. An iterative solver may return only an approximate solution, with its usefulness depending on conditioning, preconditioning, and a stopping tolerance.

This chapter develops the numerical-linear-algebra language needed to reason about those choices. We begin with residuals and errors, then separate an abstract linear operator from the way it is stored or applied. We identify the matrix structures that matter to solver choice, derive the role of conditioning, and compare direct and iterative approaches. Krylov spaces provide the common viewpoint behind conjugate gradients, MINRES, and GMRES. We then introduce preconditioning, matrix-free application, block elimination, Schur complements, and the saddle-point systems that arise in mixed PDEs and constrained optimization.

The scope is deliberately practical. We derive the main identities that explain why the methods work and what assumptions they require, but we do not develop the complete convergence theory of Krylov methods, advanced spectral estimates, multigrid or domain-decomposition construction, or parallel sparse linear algebra. The companion [04a · Further numerical linear algebra for PDE-constrained optimization](04a-further-linear-algebra-for-pde-optimization.md) collects a small set of extensions that are especially useful for later PDE-control material: families of mesh-dependent systems, spectral equivalence, block KKT preconditioning, mesh/parameter robustness, and inexact inner solves.

## Before you start

This chapter assumes:

- elementary linear algebra: matrices, transposes, linear systems, eigenvalues, and Euclidean inner products;
- [03 · Finite elements](03-finite-elements.md) for the origin of stiffness, mass, and coupling matrices, especially if you want the PDE examples to be concrete.

The duality language from [02 · Duality, derivatives, and adjoints](02-duality-derivatives-and-adjoints.md) is useful when interpreting transpose actions and matrix-free operators, but the chapter is written so that the first half can be read using ordinary matrix notation.

## What you will be able to do

After this chapter, you should be able to:

- distinguish a linear operator from a dense, sparse, or matrix-free representation of that operator;
- define the algebraic residual and relate it to the solution error;
- recognize symmetry, positive definiteness, nonsymmetry, singularity, and indefiniteness as solver-relevant structure;
- explain why finite-element matrices are often sparse and why sparse direct factorization can nevertheless become expensive;
- define a matrix condition number and derive its role in sensitivity to perturbations;
- explain the difference between a direct factorization and an iterative solve;
- interpret a Krylov method as searching in an expanding space generated by repeated operator actions;
- derive the basic conjugate-gradient iteration for a symmetric positive-definite system;
- explain why MINRES is suited to symmetric-indefinite systems and why GMRES is used for general nonsymmetric systems;
- explain what a preconditioner is and why applying one should be much cheaper than solving the original problem exactly;
- understand how a matrix-free method can apply a finite-element operator without storing its global matrix;
- derive a Schur complement and use it to interpret block systems;
- recognize the algebraic structure of mixed and KKT saddle-point systems;
- interpret iterative-solver tolerances and reports without confusing residual size with exact solution error.

## Roadmap

We first write a linear system as an operator equation and distinguish its residual from its error. We then examine dense, sparse, and operator-only realizations before classifying the matrix structures that determine which solver families are admissible. Conditioning explains why a small perturbation or residual may be amplified by the inverse operator.

The middle of the chapter turns to algorithms. Direct factorization is contrasted with iterative approximation, after which Krylov spaces provide the common framework for conjugate gradients, MINRES, and GMRES. Preconditioning changes the linear system into an equivalent one that is easier for an iterative method. Matrix-free application shows that a Krylov method may need only operator actions rather than explicit matrix entries.

The last part returns to coupled systems. Block elimination produces Schur complements, which in turn explain both saddle-point structure and many block preconditioners. We finish by treating linear solves as numerical operations with tolerances, iteration counts, and possible failure modes rather than as exact algebraic inverses.

Readers who later need to interpret PDE-optimization preconditioners can continue with the short companion [04a · Further numerical linear algebra for PDE-constrained optimization](04a-further-linear-algebra-for-pde-optimization.md). It does not add another solver survey; it focuses only on mesh-dependent families, spectral equivalence, ideal versus approximate KKT block factorizations, robustness, and nested solves.

Three examples recur throughout:

- an SPD stiffness matrix produced by a coercive elliptic finite-element problem;
- a nonsymmetric matrix produced by adding an advective term;
- a symmetric-indefinite saddle-point matrix of mixed or KKT type.

## 1. A linear solve is an operator equation, not just a call to an inverse

Consider

$$
A x=b,
$$

with

$$
A\in\mathbb{R}^{n\times n},
\qquad
x,b\in\mathbb{R}^{n}.
$$

If $A$ is nonsingular, elementary algebra writes the exact solution as

$$
x_{\ast}=A^{-1}b.
$$

That notation is useful theoretically, but it can be misleading computationally. Numerical software almost never forms the dense inverse matrix $A^{-1}$ merely to solve one system. A direct method factors $A$ and performs triangular solves. An iterative method builds a sequence of approximations using actions of $A$ and perhaps a preconditioner. A matrix-free method may never store the entries of $A$ at all.

The operation we care about is therefore

```text
right-hand side b
      ↓
solve with A
      ↓
approximate or exact x satisfying A x ≈ b
```

rather than “compute $A^{-1}$ as a matrix.”

### 1.1 Residual and error are different objects

Let $x_{k}$ be an approximation to $x_{\ast}$. Its **algebraic residual** is

$$
r_{k}
\coloneqq
b-Ax_{k}.
$$

Its **error** is

$$
e_{k}
\coloneqq
x_{\ast}-x_{k}.
$$

Because

$$
Ax_{\ast}=b,
$$

subtracting $Ax_{k}$ gives

$$
A e_{k}=r_{k}.
$$

If $A$ is nonsingular,

$$
e_{k}=A^{-1}r_{k}.
$$

This short identity is fundamental. The residual is computable because $b$, $A$, and $x_{k}$ are known. The true error is usually not computable because $x_{\ast}$ is precisely what we are trying to find.

It also shows why a small residual does not automatically mean a small error in every norm. We have

$$
\lVert e_{k}\rVert
\leq
\lVert A^{-1}\rVert
\lVert r_{k}\rVert.
$$

If $\lVert A^{-1}\rVert$ is large, the inverse can amplify a residual substantially. Conditioning will make that statement more precise in Section 4.

### 1.2 The residual is a backward-error statement

The equation

$$
r_{k}=b-Ax_{k}
$$

can be rearranged as

$$
A x_{k}=b-r_{k}.
$$

Thus $x_{k}$ is the exact solution of a nearby system with the same matrix and a perturbed right-hand side $b-r_{k}$. A relative residual such as

$$
\frac{\lVert r_{k}\rVert}{\lVert b\rVert}
$$

therefore measures how large that right-hand-side perturbation is relative to the original data.

Whether that nearby problem has a nearby solution depends on the sensitivity of the linear system. That is why stopping criteria and conditioning cannot be understood independently.

## 2. One linear operator can have several numerical representations

[03 · Finite elements](03-finite-elements.md) constructed finite-element matrices by choosing bases and evaluating bilinear forms. For example,

$$
A_{ij}=a(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{i}).
$$

Once a basis is fixed, the matrix $A$ represents the discrete linear operator. But the matrix entries are not the operator itself. Numerically, we can realize the same action in several ways.

### 2.1 Dense storage

A dense $n\times n$ matrix stores all

$$
n^{2}
$$

entries, including zeros.

Dense storage is natural for small problems and for matrices with no exploitable sparsity. It is usually a poor representation for a large finite-element operator because each basis function interacts only with basis functions whose supports overlap.

### 2.2 Sparse storage

A sparse representation stores only entries known or expected to be nonzero, together with indexing information that records where those entries belong.

For a finite-element stiffness matrix, [03 · Finite elements](03-finite-elements.md) showed that

$$
A_{ij}=0
$$

whenever the supports of $\varphi_{i}$ and $\varphi_{j}$ do not meet. Since local basis functions overlap only with a limited neighborhood, the number of nonzero entries grows much more slowly than $n^{2}$.

Sparse storage makes operations such as

$$
y=Ax
$$

possible without touching an entire dense array.

Sparsity does **not** imply that every algorithm remains sparse. A factorization can create new nonzero entries in positions where $A$ itself had zeros. This phenomenon, called **fill-in**, is one reason sparse direct methods can consume much more memory than the original matrix.

### 2.3 Operator-only representation

An iterative algorithm may not need access to individual entries $A_{ij}$. It may need only a routine that computes

$$
v\longmapsto Av.
$$

We can therefore regard the numerical object as an action

$$
\mathcal A\colon
\mathbb{R}^{n}\to\mathbb{R}^{n}
$$

without requiring an explicitly stored global matrix.

This is the basic setting for **matrix-free** methods, discussed in Section 10. It is also the natural viewpoint for Jacobian-vector products in nonlinear problems: a Krylov solver can act on the derivative operator even when no Jacobian matrix has been assembled.

The representation choice and the mathematical operator should therefore be kept separate:

```text
linear operator A
      │
      ├─► dense matrix
      ├─► sparse matrix
      └─► action v ↦ A v
```

Different representations can expose the same mathematical map to the solver.

## 3. Matrix structure determines which solver assumptions are valid

Not every nonsingular matrix should be treated the same way. Several structural properties have direct consequences for numerical methods.

### 3.1 Symmetry

A real square matrix is **symmetric** when

$$
A=A^{\mathsf T}.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
x^{\mathsf T}Ay =
y^{\mathsf T}Ax
$$

for every $x,y\in\mathbb{R}^{n}$.

A finite-element matrix inherits symmetry when the underlying bilinear form is symmetric. For the Poisson bilinear form

$$
a(v,w) =
\int_{\Omega}\nabla v\cdot\nabla w\thinspace\mathrm{d}x,
$$

we have

$$
a(v,w)=a(w,v),
$$

and therefore the stiffness matrix is symmetric.

### 3.2 Positive definiteness

A symmetric matrix $A$ is **positive definite** when

$$
x^{\mathsf T}Ax\gt0
\qquad
\text{for every }x\ne0.
$$

A symmetric positive-definite matrix is abbreviated **SPD**.

For a conforming Poisson discretization with homogeneous Dirichlet conditions, let

$$
v_{h} =
\sum_{j=1}^{n}x_{j}\varphi_{j}.
$$

Then

$$
\begin{aligned}
x^{\mathsf T}Ax
&=
\sum_{i,j=1}^{n}
x_{i}A_{ij}x_{j}
\\
&=
a(v_{h},v_{h})
\\
&=
\int_{\Omega}
\lvert\nabla v_{h}\rvert^{2}
\thinspace\mathrm{d}x.
\end{aligned}
$$

If $x\ne0$, then $v_{h}$ is not the zero function. Because the homogeneous Dirichlet space contains no nonzero constant functions, Poincaré's inequality implies

$$
\int_{\Omega}\lvert\nabla v_{h}\rvert^{2}\thinspace\mathrm{d}x\gt0.
$$

Thus the discrete stiffness matrix is SPD.

This is not merely a pleasant theoretical property. It is exactly the structure required by the standard conjugate-gradient method.

### 3.3 Nonsymmetry

A matrix is nonsymmetric when

$$
A\ne A^{\mathsf T}.
$$

For example, an advection-diffusion weak form may contain

$$
\int_{\Omega}(\boldsymbol\beta\cdot\nabla y)v\thinspace\mathrm{d}x.
$$

The associated contribution is generally not symmetric under exchange of trial and test functions. The assembled matrix therefore loses the symmetry of pure diffusion.

This matters because methods such as CG and MINRES rely on symmetry. A general nonsymmetric system usually calls for a different Krylov method, such as GMRES.

### 3.4 Singularity

A square matrix is **singular** when there exists a nonzero vector $z$ such that

$$
Az=0.
$$

The vector $z$ belongs to the nullspace or kernel of $A$.

The pure-Neumann Poisson problem from [01 · Function spaces and weak PDEs](01-function-spaces-and-weak-pdes.md) provides a PDE example. Constant functions have zero gradient, so the associated stiffness operator has a constant nullspace unless a normalization removes it. A compatible right-hand side may still admit solutions, but they are not unique until the nullspace is fixed.

A singular system therefore requires more than choosing a generic solver. One must understand compatibility, nullspaces, quotient spaces, constraints, or regularization as appropriate to the problem.

### 3.5 Indefiniteness

A symmetric matrix is **indefinite** when its quadratic form takes both signs: there exist vectors $x$ and $y$ such that

$$
x^{\mathsf T}Ax\gt0,
\qquad
 y^{\mathsf T}Ay\lt0.
$$

Saddle-point and KKT systems are common symmetric-indefinite systems. They may be perfectly nonsingular while failing the positivity assumption required by CG.

The important lesson is that “symmetric” and “positive definite” are separate properties:

```text
symmetric + positive definite     → CG is a natural candidate
symmetric + indefinite            → MINRES is a natural candidate
nonsymmetric                      → GMRES is a common candidate
singular                          → analyze compatibility/nullspace first
```

These are structural eligibility statements, not guarantees that the listed solver will be efficient without a good preconditioner.

## 4. Conditioning measures sensitivity of the solution map

Suppose $A$ is nonsingular. The exact solution is

$$
x=A^{-1}b.
$$

Fix a vector norm $\lVert\cdot\rVert$. The corresponding **induced matrix norm** is

$$
\lVert A\rVert
\coloneqq
\sup_{z\ne0}
\frac{\lVert Az\rVert}{\lVert z\rVert}.
$$

It is the smallest constant satisfying

$$
\lVert Az\rVert
\leq
\lVert A\rVert
\lVert z\rVert
\qquad
\text{for every }z.
$$

Perturb only the right-hand side:

$$
b\mapsto b+\delta b.
$$

The perturbed solution is

$$
x+\delta x =
A^{-1}(b+\delta b),
$$

so

$$
\delta x=A^{-1}\delta b.
$$

Using an induced matrix norm,

$$
\lVert\delta x\rVert
\leq
\lVert A^{-1}\rVert
\lVert\delta b\rVert.
$$

To turn this into a relative estimate, note that

$$
b=Ax,
$$

hence

$$
\lVert b\rVert
\leq
\lVert A\rVert
\lVert x\rVert,
$$

or

$$
\frac{1}{\lVert x\rVert}
\leq
\frac{\lVert A\rVert}{\lVert b\rVert}.
$$

Combining the two bounds gives

$$
\frac{\lVert\delta x\rVert}{\lVert x\rVert}
\leq
\lVert A\rVert
\lVert A^{-1}\rVert
\frac{\lVert\delta b\rVert}{\lVert b\rVert}.
$$

This motivates the **condition number**

$$
\kappa(A)
\coloneqq
\lVert A\rVert
\lVert A^{-1}\rVert.
$$

A large condition number means that the solution map can strongly amplify relative perturbations.

### 4.1 The two-norm condition number

For the Euclidean vector norm, the singular values of $A$ are the nonnegative square roots of the eigenvalues of

$$
A^{\mathsf T}A.
$$

Order them as

$$
\sigma_{\max}(A)
\geq
\cdots
\geq
\sigma_{\min}(A)
\geq0.
$$

The induced two-norm satisfies

$$
\lVert A\rVert_{2}=\sigma_{\max}(A).
$$

If $A$ is nonsingular, then $\sigma_{\min}(A)\gt0$ and

$$
\lVert A^{-1}\rVert_{2} =
\frac{1}{\sigma_{\min}(A)}.
$$

Therefore

$$
\kappa_{2}(A) =
\frac{\sigma_{\max}(A)}{\sigma_{\min}(A)}.
$$

For an SPD matrix, the singular values are its positive eigenvalues, so

$$
\kappa_{2}(A) =
\frac{\lambda_{\max}(A)}{\lambda_{\min}(A)}.
$$

A large ratio means that the operator acts with very different strength in different directions.

### 4.2 Conditioning also relates residual and error

Return to

$$
Ae_{k}=r_{k}.
$$

The same argument gives

$$
\lVert e_{k}\rVert
\leq
\lVert A^{-1}\rVert
\lVert r_{k}\rVert.
$$

Moreover,

$$
\lVert b\rVert =
\lVert Ax_{\ast}\rVert
\leq
\lVert A\rVert
\lVert x_{\ast}\rVert.
$$

Therefore

$$
\frac{\lVert e_{k}\rVert}{\lVert x_{\ast}\rVert}
\leq
\kappa(A)
\frac{\lVert r_{k}\rVert}{\lVert b\rVert}.
$$

The bound may be pessimistic, but it makes the key point explicit: a residual tolerance has to be interpreted together with the conditioning of the system and with the norm in which the residual is measured.

### 4.3 Conditioning depends on representation

The condition number belongs to a matrix together with a chosen norm and coordinate representation. Rescaling variables or changing basis can change the numerical conditioning even when the underlying abstract equation represents the same physical problem.

Finite-element matrices illustrate this clearly. Refinement changes not only the dimension of the space but also the coefficient representation of the differential operator. The stiffness system generally becomes harder for an unpreconditioned iterative method as the mesh is refined.

A good preconditioner can be viewed partly as constructing a better numerical coordinate system for the solve: it transforms the system so that the iterative method sees a more favorable operator without changing the desired physical solution.

### 4.4 Conditioning and algorithmic stability are different questions

Conditioning is a property of the mathematical problem. It asks how strongly the exact solution can change when the input data are perturbed. For the linear system

$$
Ax=b,
$$

the condition number describes the sensitivity of the exact solution map $b\mapsto A^{-1}b$ in the chosen norm and coordinates.

**Algorithmic stability** asks a different question: how much additional error is introduced by the numerical procedure used to compute an approximation in finite precision? A well-conditioned problem can still be solved poorly by an unstable algorithm. Conversely, if the mathematical problem is severely ill-conditioned, no stable algorithm can in general recover information that tiny perturbations in the data have already made ambiguous.

This distinction also clarifies the backward-error interpretation from Section 1.2. A small residual shows that the computed vector solves a nearby problem with a perturbed right-hand side. It does not, by itself, prove that every intermediate operation of the solver was stable. Full floating-point and backward-stability analysis belongs to numerical analysis more broadly; for the purposes of this chapter, the important point is to keep **problem sensitivity** and **algorithm behavior** conceptually separate.

## 5. Direct and iterative methods make different trade-offs

There are two broad ways to solve a linear system.

### 5.1 Direct methods

A direct method transforms the matrix into factors from which the solution can be obtained by a finite sequence of algebraic operations, up to floating-point roundoff.

For a general square matrix, the basic model is an LU factorization,

$$
PA=LU,
$$

with a permutation $P$, a lower-triangular factor $L$, and an upper-triangular factor $U$.

For an SPD matrix, Cholesky factorization uses

$$
A=LL^{\mathsf T}.
$$

For symmetric-indefinite systems, symmetry-preserving factorizations use variants of

$$
P^{\mathsf T}AP=LDL^{\mathsf T}
$$

with pivoting and block-diagonal $D$.

Once the factorization is available, several right-hand sides can often be solved efficiently. This makes direct methods attractive for moderate problem sizes or when robustness is more important than memory.

The difficulty is scale. Sparse PDE matrices may be cheap to store initially, yet their factors can contain substantial fill-in. Factorization memory and work can therefore grow much faster than the storage cost of the original sparse matrix.

### 5.2 Iterative methods

An iterative method produces a sequence

$$
x_{0},x_{1},x_{2},\ldots
$$

intended to approach the solution. It stops when a convergence test is satisfied or when a work limit is reached.

The attraction is that one iteration may require only:

- one or a few matrix-vector products;
- vector updates and inner products;
- applications of a preconditioner.

If these operations scale nearly linearly with the number of unknowns, very large sparse systems become accessible without a global factorization.

The price is that convergence is not automatic. It depends on the matrix structure, spectrum or more general operator geometry, the initial guess, the preconditioner, finite-precision effects, and the stopping criterion.

Classical **stationary iterations** such as Jacobi and Gauss–Seidel also belong to the iterative family. They repeatedly apply the same fixed update derived from a splitting of $A$. They are important building blocks, and variants such as Jacobi, Gauss–Seidel, or SSOR often appear later as preconditioners or multigrid smoothers. They are not developed here as the main large-scale outer solvers because the project-facing material relies more directly on Krylov methods.

### 5.3 Repeated solves change the cost calculation

PDE-constrained optimization rarely solves one isolated system. A reduced method may solve the state equation and an adjoint equation at every outer iteration. A Newton method may solve a linearized system repeatedly. A block preconditioner may itself contain approximate state solves.

The relevant cost is therefore often not

> How expensive is one solve?

but rather

> How much setup can be reused, how much work does each application cost, and how many times will this operation be called?

A factorization with high setup cost may be competitive if it is reused many times. A matrix-free Krylov solve may be preferable when assembling or factoring the matrix would dominate the total work. Numerical linear algebra has to be judged in the context of the surrounding algorithm.

## 6. Krylov spaces provide a common language for iterative solvers

Start from an initial guess $x_{0}$ and residual

$$
r_{0}=b-Ax_{0}.
$$

The $m$-th **Krylov space** generated by $A$ and $r_{0}$ is

$$
\mathcal K_{m}(A,r_{0})
\coloneqq
\mathop{\mathrm{span}}
\left\lbrace
r_{0},
Ar_{0},
A^{2}r_{0},
\ldots,
A^{m-1}r_{0}
\right\rbrace.
$$

Many important iterative methods choose

$$
x_{m}
\in
x_{0}+\mathcal K_{m}(A,r_{0})
$$

according to a method-specific optimality or orthogonality condition.

The remarkable point is that the space is generated using repeated actions of $A$. No basis of eigenvectors and no explicit inverse are required.

### 6.1 Krylov iterations implicitly construct polynomials of the matrix

Any vector in $\mathcal K_{m}(A,r_{0})$ can be written as

$$
q_{m-1}(A)r_{0}
$$

for some polynomial $q_{m-1}$ of degree at most $m-1$. Therefore a Krylov iterate has the form

$$
x_{m} =
x_{0}+q_{m-1}(A)r_{0}.
$$

Its residual is

$$
\begin{aligned}
r_{m}
&=
b-Ax_{m}
\\
&=
r_{0}-Aq_{m-1}(A)r_{0}
\\
&=
p_{m}(A)r_{0},
\end{aligned}
$$

where

$$
p_{m}(t)
\coloneqq
1-tq_{m-1}(t).
$$

Thus

$$
p_{m}(0)=1.
$$

A Krylov method is, in part, choosing a polynomial that damps the components of the initial residual seen through the operator.

This viewpoint helps explain why eigenvalue distribution and preconditioning matter. If the transformed operator has a spectrum that can be well approximated by a low-degree residual polynomial, convergence can be rapid. For nonsymmetric matrices, eigenvalues alone do not tell the whole story. This is especially true for **nonnormal** matrices, for which

$$
A^{\mathsf T}A
\ne
AA^{\mathsf T}.
$$

The polynomial picture is therefore a guide rather than a complete convergence theory.

### 6.2 Different methods impose different conditions inside the same search space

The main methods used later differ in how they choose the best point in

$$
x_{0}+\mathcal K_{m}(A,r_{0}).
$$

- CG exploits SPD structure and minimizes the error in the $A$-energy norm.
- MINRES exploits symmetry and minimizes a residual norm without requiring positive definiteness.
- GMRES works for general matrices and minimizes the Euclidean residual norm over the Krylov space.

The shared Krylov structure is why all three methods can be implemented from repeated operator actions rather than explicit inversion.

## 7. Conjugate gradients for symmetric positive-definite systems

Consider

$$
Ax=b
$$

with $A$ SPD.

The system is equivalent to minimizing the quadratic function

$$
\phi(x)
\coloneqq
\frac12 x^{\mathsf T}Ax-b^{\mathsf T}x.
$$

Indeed,

$$
\nabla\phi(x)=Ax-b.
$$

Therefore

$$
\nabla\phi(x)=0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
Ax=b.
$$

Because $A$ is positive definite, $\phi$ is strictly convex and has a unique minimizer $x_{\ast}$.

If we define

$$
r_{k}\coloneqq b-Ax_{k},
$$

then

$$
r_{k}=-\nabla\phi(x_{k}).
$$

So the residual is the negative gradient of the quadratic.

### 7.1 Exact minimization along one direction

Suppose we have a search direction $d_{k}$ and consider

$$
x_{k+1} =
x_{k}+\alpha d_{k}.
$$

Along this line,

$$
\psi(\alpha)
\coloneqq
\phi(x_{k}+\alpha d_{k}).
$$

Differentiate:

$$
\begin{aligned}
\psi'(\alpha)
&=
d_{k}^{\mathsf T}
\left(A(x_{k}+\alpha d_{k})-b\right)
\\
&=
-d_{k}^{\mathsf T}r_{k}
+
\alpha d_{k}^{\mathsf T}Ad_{k}.
\end{aligned}
$$

Setting $\psi'(\alpha)=0$ gives

$$
\alpha_{k} =
\frac{d_{k}^{\mathsf T}r_{k}}
{d_{k}^{\mathsf T}Ad_{k}}.
$$

Because $A$ is positive definite,

$$
d_{k}^{\mathsf T}Ad_{k}\gt0
$$

for every nonzero direction, so the denominator is safe in exact arithmetic.

CG constructs the directions so that

$$
d_{k}^{\mathsf T}r_{k}=r_{k}^{\mathsf T}r_{k},
$$

which gives the familiar formula

$$
\alpha_{k} =
\frac{r_{k}^{\mathsf T}r_{k}}
{d_{k}^{\mathsf T}Ad_{k}}.
$$

### 7.2 Conjugate directions remove quadratic cross-coupling

Two directions $d_{i}$ and $d_{j}$ are **$A$-conjugate** when

$$
d_{i}^{\mathsf T}A d_{j}=0.
$$

This is orthogonality in the inner product induced by the SPD matrix:

$$
(v,w)_{A}\coloneqq v^{\mathsf T}Aw.
$$

Why is this useful? Suppose an update contains components along two directions,

$$
s=\alpha d_{i}+\beta d_{j}.
$$

The quadratic part contains the cross term

$$
\alpha\beta d_{i}^{\mathsf T}Ad_{j}.
$$

If the directions are $A$-conjugate, that term vanishes. Minimizing along one conjugate direction does not reintroduce quadratic coupling with a previously treated conjugate direction.

### 7.3 The three-term recurrence

CG begins with

$$
d_{0}=r_{0}.
$$

After taking the step

$$
x_{k+1}=x_{k}+\alpha_{k}d_{k},
$$

the residual can be updated without recomputing $Ax_{k+1}$ from scratch:

$$
r_{k+1} =
r_{k}-\alpha_{k}Ad_{k}.
$$

The next direction is constructed from the new residual and the previous direction:

$$
d_{k+1} =
r_{k+1}+\beta_{k}d_{k}.
$$

The coefficient $\beta_{k}$ is chosen to maintain conjugacy with the previous direction:

$$
d_{k+1}^{\mathsf T}A d_{k}=0.
$$

Substituting

$$
d_{k+1}=r_{k+1}+\beta_{k}d_{k}
$$

gives first

$$
\beta_{k} =
-\frac{r_{k+1}^{\mathsf T}A d_{k}}
{d_{k}^{\mathsf T}A d_{k}}.
$$

Now use the residual update

$$
A d_{k} =
\frac{r_{k}-r_{k+1}}{\alpha_{k}}.
$$

At the minimizing step length, the line derivative vanishes. Using the residual at the new point,

$$
\psi'(\alpha_{k}) =
-d_{k}^{\mathsf T}r_{k+1} =
0.
$$

The conjugate-direction construction extends this orthogonality to all search directions already generated. Since those directions span the same Krylov space in which the earlier residuals lie, successive residuals are orthogonal in exact arithmetic:

$$
r_{k+1}^{\mathsf T}r_{k}=0.
$$

Therefore

$$
\begin{aligned}
-r_{k+1}^{\mathsf T}A d_{k}
&=
-\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}
r_{k+1}^{\mathsf T}(r_{k}-r_{k+1})
\\
&=
\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}
r_{k+1}^{\mathsf T}r_{k+1}.
\end{aligned}
$$

From the formula for $\alpha_{k}$,

$$
d_{k}^{\mathsf T}A d_{k} =
\frac{1}{\alpha_{k}}
r_{k}^{\mathsf T}r_{k}.
$$

Hence

$$
\beta_{k} =
\frac{r_{k+1}^{\mathsf T}r_{k+1}}
{r_{k}^{\mathsf T}r_{k}}.
$$

The complete unpreconditioned iteration is therefore

```text
r_0 = b - A x_0
d_0 = r_0

for k = 0, 1, ...
    α_k = (r_k^T r_k) / (d_k^T A d_k)
    x_{k+1} = x_k + α_k d_k
    r_{k+1} = r_k - α_k A d_k
    β_k = (r_{k+1}^T r_{k+1}) / (r_k^T r_k)
    d_{k+1} = r_{k+1} + β_k d_k
```

Only one new multiplication by $A$ is needed per iteration if $Ad_{k}$ is reused.

### 7.4 What CG is optimizing

Let

$$
e_{k}=x_{\ast}-x_{k}.
$$

The SPD matrix defines the energy norm

$$
\lVert e\rVert_{A}
\coloneqq
\sqrt{e^{\mathsf T}Ae}.
$$

CG chooses $x_{m}$ so that, in exact arithmetic,

$$
\lVert x_{\ast}-x_{m}\rVert_{A} =
\min_{x\in x_{0}+\mathcal K_{m}(A,r_{0})}
\lVert x_{\ast}-x\rVert_{A}.
$$

Thus CG is not merely a gradient method with a clever step length. It constructs an expanding Krylov space and finds the best approximation in the energy norm within that space.

In exact arithmetic, an $n$-dimensional SPD system is solved in at most $n$ CG steps. In practice, finite precision and large $n$ make that statement less useful than the rate at which an acceptable residual is reached. Preconditioning is therefore central to practical CG.

### 7.5 Why CG fails as a general-purpose solver

The recurrence above repeatedly uses positivity. In particular,

$$
d_{k}^{\mathsf T}Ad_{k}\gt0
$$

must hold for nonzero $d_{k}$. If $A$ is symmetric indefinite, this denominator may be zero or negative, and the quadratic $\phi$ is no longer strictly convex.

If $A$ is nonsymmetric, the $A$-inner-product interpretation disappears entirely.

CG is powerful precisely because it exploits strong structure. Using it on a system that does not satisfy that structure is not a harmless approximation; it invalidates the method's mathematical basis.

## 8. MINRES and GMRES extend Krylov ideas beyond SPD systems

The Krylov-space construction survives when CG's positivity assumption does not. What changes is the condition used to select the iterate.

### 8.1 MINRES: symmetric but not necessarily positive definite

Suppose

$$
A=A^{\mathsf T},
$$

but $A$ may be indefinite.

Symmetry still allows the Krylov basis to be generated by the **Lanczos process**, whose projected matrix is tridiagonal. Positive definiteness is no longer available, so we do not use the CG energy-minimization argument.

MINRES instead chooses an iterate in

$$
x_{0}+\mathcal K_{m}(A,r_{0})
$$

that minimizes the Euclidean residual norm,

$$
\lVert b-Ax_{m}\rVert_{2}.
$$

Because the Lanczos projection is tridiagonal, MINRES can update this minimization with short recurrences rather than storing an ever-growing dense Krylov basis.

This makes MINRES a natural method for nonsingular symmetric-indefinite systems such as many saddle-point and KKT matrices.

The word “symmetric” remains essential. A preconditioner used with MINRES must preserve the appropriate self-adjoint structure; in the standard SPD-preconditioned formulation, this is why one asks for a symmetric positive-definite preconditioner rather than an arbitrary approximate inverse.

### 8.2 GMRES: general nonsymmetric systems

For a general matrix $A$, the **Arnoldi process** constructs an orthonormal basis

$$
V_{m} =
\begin{bmatrix}
v_{1}&\cdots&v_{m}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

for the Krylov space. Its central relation is

$$
A V_{m} =
V_{m+1}\overline H_{m},
$$

where $\overline H_{m}$ is a small **upper-Hessenberg matrix**, meaning that its entries below the first subdiagonal are zero.

Let

$$
r_{0}=b-Ax_{0},
\qquad
\beta=\lVert r_{0}\rVert_{2},
\qquad
v_{1}=\frac{r_{0}}{\beta}.
$$

Every candidate in the affine Krylov space can be written

$$
x_{m} =
x_{0}+V_{m}y
$$

for some $y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}$. Its residual is

$$
\begin{aligned}
r_{m}
&=
b-A(x_{0}+V_{m}y)
\\
&=
r_{0}-AV_{m}y
\\
&=
V_{m+1}
\left(
\beta e_{1}-\overline H_{m}y
\right).
\end{aligned}
$$

Because the columns of $V_{m+1}$ are orthonormal,

$$
\lVert r_{m}\rVert_{2} =
\left\lVert
\beta e_{1}-\overline H_{m}y
\right\rVert_{2}.
$$

GMRES therefore reduces the large residual-minimization problem to the small least-squares problem

$$
\min_{y\in\mathbb{R}^{m}}
\left\lVert
\beta e_{1}-\overline H_{m}y
\right\rVert_{2}.
$$

This is the defining idea of the **generalized minimal residual method**.

### 8.3 Why GMRES costs more memory than CG or MINRES

For a general nonsymmetric matrix, Arnoldi orthogonalizes each new Krylov vector against the previous basis vectors. Unrestarted GMRES therefore stores an expanding basis and performs increasingly many inner products as $m$ grows.

A common practical remedy is **restarted GMRES**: after a fixed number of Arnoldi vectors, retain the current iterate, discard the basis, and begin a new Krylov cycle. This limits memory and orthogonalization work, but restarting can slow convergence because information in the discarded basis is lost.

Thus there is no universally superior Krylov method. The strongest exploitable structure usually leads to the cheapest suitable algorithm:

```text
SPD                         → CG
symmetric indefinite        → MINRES
general nonsymmetric        → GMRES or another nonsymmetric Krylov method
```

## 9. Preconditioning changes the system seen by the iterative method

An iterative method can be mathematically appropriate and still converge far too slowly. **Preconditioning** replaces the original system by an equivalent one whose operator is easier for the iterative method.

Let $M$ be a matrix that approximates $A$ in some useful sense and is much cheaper to solve with.

A left-preconditioned system is

$$
M^{-1}Ax =
M^{-1}b.
$$

A right-preconditioned system introduces

$$
x=M^{-1}y
$$

and solves

$$
AM^{-1}y=b.
$$

In either case, the desired physical solution $x$ is unchanged after the appropriate transformation.

### 9.1 A preconditioner is usually an action, not an explicit inverse

The notation $M^{-1}$ should again not be read as “form the inverse matrix.” Applying a preconditioner normally means:

> given a vector $r$, compute an inexpensive approximation $z$ to the solution of
>
> $$
> Mz=r.
> $$

That action may be as simple as division by the diagonal entries of $A$, or as sophisticated as a few multigrid cycles.

The ideal choice

$$
M=A
$$

would give

$$
M^{-1}A=I,
$$

so an iterative method would have nothing difficult left to solve. But applying $M^{-1}$ would then be exactly as hard as solving the original system. A practical preconditioner balances two goals:

1. make the transformed operator much easier for the outer iteration;
2. keep each preconditioner application cheap.

### 9.2 Diagonal scaling as the simplest example

Suppose

$$
A=
\begin{bmatrix}
10^{6}&0\\
0&1
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Its two-norm condition number is

$$
\kappa_{2}(A)=10^{6}.
$$

Choose the diagonal preconditioner

$$
M=A.
$$

Then the transformed matrix is the identity. This example is deliberately trivial, but it exposes the purpose of scaling: the original coefficient directions had vastly different magnitudes, and the preconditioner normalizes them.

For a genuine PDE matrix, the ideal scaling is not usually available as cheaply as in this diagonal example. The same principle remains: construct an inexpensive operator that captures the difficult part of $A$ well enough to reduce the work of the outer Krylov method.

### 9.3 Preconditioning must respect solver structure

Preconditioning is not independent of the chosen Krylov method.

For SPD CG, the standard preconditioner is itself SPD. Preconditioned CG can then be interpreted through an equivalent symmetrically transformed system and retains the short conjugate-gradient recurrence.

For MINRES, preserving symmetry is equally important. A general left preconditioner may make

$$
M^{-1}A
$$

nonsymmetric in the Euclidean inner product even when both $A$ and $M$ are symmetric. The proper preconditioned formulation therefore uses the SPD preconditioner together with the corresponding weighted inner product or symmetric transformation.

GMRES has fewer structural restrictions and can use left or right preconditioning for general nonsymmetric systems. If the preconditioner itself changes from one outer iteration to the next, a **flexible** Krylov variant such as flexible GMRES may be required.

For SPD systems, the condition number of the preconditioned operator is a useful guide to CG convergence. For general nonsymmetric systems, however, one should not reduce preconditioner quality to a condition number alone: eigenvalue clustering and nonnormal operator behavior can also matter. The practical lesson needed here is modest: a good preconditioner makes the operator easier for the **chosen** Krylov method, and what “easier” means depends on that method and matrix structure.

### 9.4 Good preconditioning is problem dependent

A preconditioner should exploit the origin of the matrix rather than treating every sparse array as unrelated data.

Examples include:

- diagonal or lumped-mass approximations for mass matrices;
- incomplete factorizations for sparse matrices;
- multigrid approximations for elliptic operators;
- domain-decomposition methods;
- block preconditioners for coupled saddle-point systems.

The last two families are important enough to have large theories of their own. This chapter uses their purpose but does not develop their construction.

## 10. Matrix-free application: compute $Av$ without assembling $A$

Finite-element structure makes the operator-only viewpoint especially concrete.

Suppose

$$
A_{ij} =
a(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{i})
$$

and let

$$
v_{h} =
\sum_{j=1}^{n}v_{j}\varphi_{j}.
$$

Then the $i$-th component of $Av$ is

$$
\begin{aligned}
(Av)_{i}
&=
\sum_{j=1}^{n}A_{ij}v_{j}
\\
&=
\sum_{j=1}^{n}
a(\varphi_{j},\varphi_{i})v_{j}
\\
&=
a(v_{h},\varphi_{i}).
\end{aligned}
$$

For the Poisson operator,

$$
(Av)_{i} =
\int_{\Omega}
\nabla v_{h}\cdot\nabla\varphi_{i}
\thinspace\mathrm{d}x.
$$

Instead of first assembling every $A_{ij}$, a matrix-free implementation can evaluate this action cell by cell:

```text
input coefficient vector v
        ↓
for each cell K
    gather local coefficients
    evaluate v_h and/or ∇v_h at quadrature points
    evaluate local operator action
    scatter local result into output vector
        ↓
return A v
```

The same local finite-element ingredients used for assembly are reused, but the global sparse matrix is never stored.

### 10.1 Why matrix-free methods can be attractive

Matrix-free application can reduce memory traffic and storage, especially for high-order finite elements where the number of matrix entries per cell grows rapidly. It also fits nonlinear problems naturally when the derivative is available as a Jacobian-vector product rather than as an assembled Jacobian.

The trade-off is that some operations become harder:

- a direct factorization is impossible without an assembled matrix;
- entry-based diagnostics are unavailable;
- building a strong preconditioner may require a separate approximate matrix or another operator-specific construction.

Matrix-free therefore does **not** mean “no matrices anywhere.” A common strategy is to apply the expensive operator matrix-free while using an assembled or simplified operator inside the preconditioner.

### 10.2 Transpose actions may require their own implementation

For a symmetric operator,

$$
A^{\mathsf T}v=Av.
$$

For a nonsymmetric operator, the transpose action is a different map. A matrix-free implementation that supports both primal and adjoint solves may therefore need separate routines for

$$
v\mapsto Av
$$

and

$$
q\mapsto A^{\mathsf T}q.
$$

This is the linear-algebra counterpart of the JVP/VJP distinction developed in [02 · Duality, derivatives, and adjoints](02-duality-derivatives-and-adjoints.md).

## 11. Block systems expose coupled structure

Many PDE and optimization systems contain several groups of unknowns. Instead of flattening everything immediately into one unstructured matrix, it is useful to retain the block form.

Consider

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
A & B^{\mathsf T}\\
B & -C
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
p
\end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix}
f\\
g
\end{bmatrix},
$$

where $A$ is assumed nonsingular for the moment.

The equations are

$$
\begin{aligned}
Ax+B^{\mathsf T}p&=f,
\\
Bx-Cp&=g.
\end{aligned}
$$

### 11.1 Eliminate the first block

From the first equation,

$$
x =
A^{-1}(f-B^{\mathsf T}p).
$$

Substitute this into the second equation:

$$
\begin{aligned}
B A^{-1}(f-B^{\mathsf T}p)-Cp
&=g,
\\
B A^{-1}f
-\left(BA^{-1}B^{\mathsf T}+C\right)p
&=g.
\end{aligned}
$$

Define the **Schur complement**

$$
S
\coloneqq
C+BA^{-1}B^{\mathsf T}.
$$

Then $p$ satisfies

$$
Sp =
BA^{-1}f-g.
$$

After solving for $p$, recover

$$
x=A^{-1}(f-B^{\mathsf T}p).
$$

This is exact block elimination.

### 11.2 The Schur complement is usually an operator, not a matrix we want to form

Even if $A$ and $B$ are sparse, the explicit matrix

$$
BA^{-1}B^{\mathsf T}
$$

is generally dense. Forming it can destroy the sparsity we began with.

But applying $S$ to a vector $q$ can be organized as

```text
q
↓
B^T q
↓
solve A z = B^T q
↓
B z
↓
add C q
```

So the Schur complement can be used as a matrix-free block operator whose application contains an inner solve with $A$.

This observation is central to block preconditioning: rather than constructing the exact Schur complement, build an inexpensive approximation whose inverse captures its important scaling and coupling.

### 11.3 The block factorization makes the same structure explicit

With

$$
S=C+BA^{-1}B^{\mathsf T},
$$

the block matrix has the exact factorization

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
A & B^{\mathsf T}\\
B & -C
\end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix}
I & 0\\
BA^{-1} & I
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
A & 0\\
0 & -S
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
I & A^{-1}B^{\mathsf T}\\
0 & I
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

This identity explains why good block preconditioners often contain approximations to two ingredients:

- the main block $A$;
- the Schur complement $S$.

The exact factorization would solve the original problem. A practical block preconditioner replaces the expensive exact inverses by cheaper approximations.

## 12. Saddle-point and KKT systems are symmetric but indefinite

The mixed finite-element companion [03a · Further finite-element notes](03a-further-finite-element-notes.md) introduced systems of the form

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
A & B^{\mathsf T}\\
B & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
p
\end{bmatrix} =
\begin{bmatrix}
f\\
g
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The same algebraic pattern appears in equality-constrained optimization. If a quadratic objective has Hessian $Q$ and equality Jacobian $D$, the associated KKT matrix is

$$
K
\coloneqq
\begin{bmatrix}
Q & D^{\mathsf T}\\
D & 0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

[06 · Constrained optimization](06-constrained-optimization.md) derives the optimization conditions that produce this matrix. Here we care only about its linear-algebra structure.

### 12.1 Why the matrix is indefinite when $Q$ is SPD and $D\ne0$

Take a vector with zero multiplier component:

$$
z_{+} =
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
0
\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
x\ne0.
$$

Then

$$
z_{+}^{\mathsf T}Kz_{+} =
x^{\mathsf T}Qx
\gt0.
$$

Now choose a nonzero multiplier $p$ for which

$$
D^{\mathsf T}p\ne0
$$

and set

$$
x=-Q^{-1}D^{\mathsf T}p.
$$

For

$$
z_{-} =
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
p
\end{bmatrix},
$$

we have

$$
\begin{aligned}
z_{-}^{\mathsf T}Kz_{-}
&=
x^{\mathsf T}Qx
+
2p^{\mathsf T}Dx
\\
&=
p^{\mathsf T}DQ^{-1}D^{\mathsf T}p
-2p^{\mathsf T}DQ^{-1}D^{\mathsf T}p
\\
&=
-p^{\mathsf T}DQ^{-1}D^{\mathsf T}p
\lt0.
\end{aligned}
$$

Thus $K$ takes both positive and negative values: it is symmetric indefinite.

This immediately rules out ordinary CG on the full KKT matrix even though the primal Hessian block $Q$ is SPD.

### 12.2 The KKT Schur complement

Assume $Q$ is SPD and $D$ has full row rank. The Schur complement is

$$
S =
DQ^{-1}D^{\mathsf T}.
$$

For any nonzero multiplier vector $p$,

$$
\begin{aligned}
p^{\mathsf T}Sp
&=
p^{\mathsf T}DQ^{-1}D^{\mathsf T}p
\\
&=
(D^{\mathsf T}p)^{\mathsf T}
Q^{-1}
(D^{\mathsf T}p).
\end{aligned}
$$

Because $Q^{-1}$ is SPD, this quantity is positive whenever

$$
D^{\mathsf T}p\ne0.
$$

Full row rank of $D$ makes $D^{\mathsf T}$ injective, so

$$
p^{\mathsf T}Sp\gt0
\qquad
\text{for every }p\ne0.
$$

Therefore the Schur complement is SPD even though the full KKT matrix is indefinite.

This is an important recurring pattern: block elimination can reveal positive-definite subproblems inside an indefinite coupled system.

### 12.3 More general saddle systems need weaker assumptions

The primal block need not be positive definite on the entire space. For a general equality-constrained problem it is often enough that the primal quadratic form be positive on feasible directions,

$$
Dz=0,
$$

together with an appropriate rank condition on $D$.

The detailed optimization interpretation belongs to [06 · Constrained optimization](06-constrained-optimization.md) and the `nmopt` manual's KKT chapter. The linear-algebra lesson is that nonsingularity of the full block system can depend on how the blocks interact, not on each diagonal block being invertible separately.

### 12.4 Solver implications

For a nonsingular symmetric-indefinite KKT system:

- CG is not appropriate for the full matrix;
- MINRES is a natural symmetry-preserving Krylov method when the preconditioner also preserves the required structure;
- GMRES remains an option and is often used when the system or preconditioner is nonsymmetric;
- block preconditioners commonly approximate the primal block and a Schur complement.

The quality of the block preconditioner often determines whether an all-at-once method is practical.

## 13. A numerical solve is approximate and must report its evidence

A direct algebraic derivation writes

$$
x=A^{-1}b
$$

as though the answer were exact. An iterative implementation instead has to decide when the current approximation is good enough.

A common residual-based criterion is

$$
\lVert r_{k}\rVert
\leq
\tau_{\mathrm{abs}}
+
\tau_{\mathrm{rel}}\lVert b\rVert,
$$

where

- $\tau_{\mathrm{abs}}$ is an absolute tolerance;
- $\tau_{\mathrm{rel}}$ is a relative tolerance.

The solver may also impose a maximum iteration count.

### 13.1 Relative and absolute tolerances answer different questions

A purely absolute threshold such as

$$
\lVert r_{k}\rVert\leq10^{-10}
$$

has different meaning when $\lVert b\rVert$ is $10^{-2}$ and when it is $10^{8}$.

A relative criterion scales with the problem magnitude. An absolute term remains useful when the right-hand side is zero or extremely small.

The combined test prevents either scale from being ignored.

### 13.2 The monitored residual must be identified

With left preconditioning, an implementation may naturally monitor the **preconditioned residual**

$$
M^{-1}(b-Ax_{k})
$$

rather than the true residual

$$
b-Ax_{k}.
$$

Those norms are not the same. Similarly, some methods have recurrence-based residual estimates that can drift from an explicitly recomputed residual in finite precision.

A solver report should therefore make clear what quantity was checked rather than reporting a number called simply “error.”

### 13.3 Inner solves should not automatically be driven to machine precision

Linear solves often occur inside another iteration:

```text
outer optimization or Newton step
        ↓
state / adjoint / Hessian / KKT solve
        ↓
return numerical result to outer method
```

If the outer iteration is still far from convergence, solving every inner system to an extremely tight tolerance may waste work. If the inner tolerance is too loose, however, the outer derivative, step, or residual may become unreliable.

This is the basic motivation for **inexact solves** and adaptive inner tolerances. Their detailed convergence theory belongs to advanced Newton–Krylov and optimization analysis, but the practical principle is already important: solve accuracy is part of the algorithm, not an implementation afterthought.

### 13.4 Iteration count is evidence, not a universal performance metric

Two solves with the same number of Krylov iterations may have very different costs if one uses a much more expensive preconditioner. Conversely, a preconditioner that costs more per iteration may reduce the iteration count enough to lower total time.

Useful solve evidence can therefore include:

- convergence or failure status;
- final residual measure;
- requested tolerance;
- iteration count;
- reason for termination;
- setup and solve work when available.

The surrounding PDE or optimization algorithm can then distinguish “a mathematical residual evaluation” from “an approximate solve with a convergence history.”

This chapter has developed the numerical-linear-algebra concepts needed by the rest of the prerequisite track:

- residual versus error;
- dense, sparse, and operator-only representations;
- solver-relevant matrix structure;
- condition numbers and sensitivity;
- direct versus iterative solves;
- Krylov spaces;
- CG for SPD systems;
- MINRES for symmetric-indefinite systems;
- GMRES for general systems;
- preconditioning;
- matrix-free operator actions;
- block elimination and Schur complements;
- saddle-point/KKT structure;
- inexact solves and stopping criteria.

Several substantial subjects are intentionally outside the core prerequisite scope.

**Detailed floating-point and stability analysis.** Backward stability of factorizations, rounding-error propagation, pivot-growth analysis, and finite-precision error bounds are important numerical-analysis topics, but the manual only needs the distinction between conditioning of a problem and stability of an algorithm.

**Detailed Krylov convergence theory.** Sharp CG bounds, detailed GMRES analysis for nonnormal matrices, breakdown theory, and finite-precision loss of orthogonality deserve a numerical-linear-algebra course of their own.

**Lanczos and Arnoldi implementation details.** We used their projected relations to explain MINRES and GMRES, but did not derive every recurrence or orthogonalization strategy.

**Multigrid and domain decomposition.** These are among the most important scalable preconditioning techniques for PDEs. Here they appear only as examples of approximate inverses. Their construction, smoothing theory, coarse spaces, and optimality theory are deferred.

**Sparse direct-solver theory.** Fill-reducing orderings, pivoting strategies, factorization data structures, and parallel sparse factorization are beyond the present purpose.

**Specialized saddle-point solvers.** Uzawa methods, projected Krylov methods, constraint preconditioners, augmented-Lagrangian preconditioners, and problem-specific Schur approximations are important extensions but not prerequisites for reading the `nmopt` manual.

**Parallel and distributed-memory linear algebra.** Partitioning, communication, ghost values, distributed sparse matrices, and scalable parallel preconditioners are deliberately outside the serial project baseline.
