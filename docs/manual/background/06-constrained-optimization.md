## Purpose

In unconstrained optimization, first-order stationarity is expressed by a simple equation: the derivative must vanish. A constraint changes that statement because not every perturbation is allowed. At a bound, for example, a nonzero derivative can be perfectly compatible with optimality if the direction of decrease points outside the admissible set.

This chapter develops the constrained-optimization language needed later for PDE-constrained optimization and bound-constrained controls. The main thread is

```text
feasible directions
    ↓
variational inequality
    ↓
projection / normal cone
    ↓
Lagrange and KKT multipliers
    ↓
complementarity
    ↓
active and inactive constraints
    ↓
primal-dual active sets
```

The emphasis is on first-order structure and on the quadratic and box-constrained cases that recur in numerical optimal control. We derive the relevant conditions rather than treating them as lists of rules.

The scope is deliberately narrower than a general nonlinear-programming course. A companion, [06a · Further notes on constrained optimization](06a-further-constrained-optimization-notes.md), collects constraint qualifications, sign conventions, second-order constrained conditions, and a short orientation to nearby methods such as penalty, augmented-Lagrangian, and SQP methods. The main chapter stays focused on the first-order structures needed later: variational inequalities, projections, KKT systems, complementarity, and active sets.

## Roadmap

We first ask what replaces the equation $f'(x)=0$ when only some directions are feasible. For a convex admissible set this leads to a variational inequality, and from there to projection and the normal cone.

We then change viewpoint and describe constraints explicitly. Equality constraints are treated geometrically first: feasible perturbations lie tangent to the constraint surface, so the objective derivative must be balanced by normal directions generated by the constraints. This leads to Lagrange multipliers and, for quadratic objectives with linear constraints, a saddle-point KKT system. Inequality constraints add multiplier signs and complementary slackness.

The final part specializes to boxes. Projection becomes componentwise clipping, the normal cone becomes a three-case sign rule, and complementarity naturally divides variables into lower-active, inactive, and upper-active sets. Once that classification is known, the remaining problem contains only equalities. Primal-dual active-set methods exploit exactly this observation.

Technical regularity assumptions, alternative sign conventions, second-order constrained conditions, and nearby general-purpose constrained algorithms are deferred to the companion notes so that they do not interrupt the main path.

## 1. Feasibility changes first-order stationarity

Consider

$$
\min_{x\in K} f(x),
$$

where $K\subset\mathbb R^{n}$ is the feasible set and $f$ is differentiable.

If $x_{\ast}$ lies in the interior of $K$, then sufficiently small perturbations in every direction are feasible. We recover ordinary unconstrained stationarity,

$$
\nabla f(x_{\ast})=0.
$$

At the boundary, however, some directions are forbidden. A nonzero gradient can then be compatible with a minimum.

Consider the scalar problem

$$
\min_{x\geq0} x.
$$

The minimizer is

$$
x_{\ast}=0,
$$

but

$$
f'(0)=1\neq0.
$$

This is not a contradiction. Moving in the descent direction $-1$ would leave the feasible set. Among feasible directions, the objective cannot decrease.

This observation is the starting point for constrained first-order conditions.

## 2. Feasible directions and the tangent cone

At a feasible point $x\in K$, a direction $d$ is locally feasible if we can approach $x$ through feasible points whose normalized displacement tends to $d$.

One convenient definition of the **tangent cone** is

$$
T_{K}(x)
:=
\left\{
 d:\
 \begin{array}{l}
 \text{there exist }t_{j}>0\text{ with }t_{j}\to0,\\
 d_{j}\to d,\text{ and }x+t_{j}d_{j}\in K
 \end{array}
\right\}.
$$

The exact technical definition matters in general nonlinear geometry. For the sets we use most often, its meaning is simpler: it contains the directions tangent to the feasible boundary or pointing into the feasible region.

For the scalar interval

$$
K=[\ell,r],
$$

we have

```math
T_{K}(x)
=
\begin{cases}
[0,\infty), & x=\ell,\\
\mathbb R, & \ell<x<r,\\
(-\infty,0], & x=r.
\end{cases}
```

Thus an interior point admits both signs, a lower-bound point admits only nonnegative first-order motion, and an upper-bound point admits only nonpositive first-order motion.

## 3. The variational inequality is constrained stationarity

Let $x_{\ast}$ be a local minimizer. For every tangent direction $d\in T_{K}(x_{\ast})$, the directional derivative cannot be negative:

$$
f'(x_{\ast})[d]\geq0.
$$

In Euclidean coordinates,

$$
\nabla f(x_{\ast})^{\mathsf T}d\geq0
\qquad
\text{for all }d\in T_{K}(x_{\ast}).
$$

For a convex set $K$, there is a particularly useful equivalent form. If $v\in K$, then the whole segment

$$
x_{\ast}+t(v-x_{\ast}),
\qquad
0\leq t\leq1,
$$

remains in $K$. Therefore $v-x_{\ast}$ is an admissible direction and

$$
\boxed{
\nabla f(x_{\ast})^{\mathsf T}(v-x_{\ast})\geq0
\qquad
\text{for every }v\in K.
}
$$

This is the **variational inequality** for the constrained problem.

If $f$ is convex, this condition is not only necessary but sufficient. Indeed, convexity gives

$$
f(v)
\geq
f(x_{\ast})
+
\nabla f(x_{\ast})^{\mathsf T}(v-x_{\ast}),
$$

and the variational inequality makes the second term nonnegative.

### 3.1 The scalar box already contains the whole sign pattern

For

$$
\ell\leq x\leq r,
$$

write

$$
g:=f'(x).
$$

The variational inequality gives

```math
\begin{cases}
g=0, & \ell<x<r,\\
g\geq0, & x=\ell,\\
g\leq0, & x=r.
\end{cases}
```

This is one of the most useful facts in the chapter. Bounds replace one stationarity equation by a switching condition.

## 4. Projection onto a closed convex set

Let $K\subset\mathbb R^{n}$ be nonempty, closed, and convex. The Euclidean projection of $z$ onto $K$ is

$$
P_{K}(z)
:=
\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{v\in K}
\frac{1}{2}\lVert v-z\rVert_{2}^{2}.
$$

For such a set the projection exists and is unique.

Suppose

$$
x=P_{K}(z).
$$

The projection itself is a constrained minimization problem with objective

$$
\varphi(v)
=
\frac{1}{2}\lVert v-z\rVert_{2}^{2}.
$$

Its gradient at $x$ is

$$
\nabla\varphi(x)=x-z.
$$

Applying the variational inequality gives

$$
(x-z)^{\mathsf T}(v-x)\geq0
\qquad
\forall v\in K.
$$

Equivalently,

$$
\boxed{
(z-x)^{\mathsf T}(v-x)\leq0
\qquad
\forall v\in K.
}
$$

This is the fundamental characterization of projection that we will reuse below.

### 4.1 Projection onto a box is clipping

For

$$
K
=
\{x:\ell_{i}\leq x_{i}\leq r_{i}\},
$$

the Euclidean projection separates componentwise:

$$
(P_{K}(z))_{i}
=
\min\{r_{i},\max\{\ell_{i},z_{i}\}\}.
$$

So projection onto a box is just clipping each coordinate to its interval.

## 5. Projected gradient and the projection fixed point

A direct way to preserve feasibility is to take an unconstrained gradient step and project it back:

$$
x_{k+1}
=
P_{K}(x_{k}-\alpha_{k}\nabla f(x_{k})).
$$

This is **projected gradient descent**.

Why is projection more than a repair operation? At a solution, the variational inequality is equivalent to a projection fixed point.

Let $\alpha>0$ and set

$$
z=x_{\ast}-\alpha\nabla f(x_{\ast}).
$$

Then

$$
z-x_{\ast}
=
-\alpha\nabla f(x_{\ast}).
$$

The projection characterization says

$$
x_{\ast}=P_{K}(z)
$$

if and only if

$$
(z-x_{\ast})^{\mathsf T}(v-x_{\ast})\leq0
\qquad
\forall v\in K.
$$

Substituting the expression for $z-x_{\ast}$ and dividing by $\alpha>0$ gives exactly

$$
\nabla f(x_{\ast})^{\mathsf T}(v-x_{\ast})\geq0.
$$

Hence

$$
\boxed{
x_{\ast}
=
P_{K}\bigl(x_{\ast}-\alpha\nabla f(x_{\ast})\bigr)
}
$$

for every $\alpha>0$ if and only if the first-order variational inequality holds.

### 5.1 Projection depends on the chosen geometry

In a space with SPD metric $G$, the natural projection is

$$
P_{K}^{G}(z)
:=
\mathop{\mathrm{argmin}}_{v\in K}
\frac{1}{2}\lVert v-z\rVert_{G}^{2},
$$

with

$$
\lVert w\rVert_{G}^{2}=w^{\mathsf T}Gw.
$$

If the derivative covector is $r=f'(x)$ and the metric gradient is $g=G^{-1}r$, the corresponding projected step is

$$
x_{k+1}
=
P_{K}^{G}(x_{k}-\alpha_{k}g_{k}).
$$

For a Euclidean box, projection is componentwise clipping. For a general non-diagonal metric, metric projection onto the same box is generally a coupled quadratic problem. This distinction becomes important when optimization geometry is not the raw coefficient geometry.

## 6. The normal cone packages the variational inequality

For a closed convex set $K$, the normal cone is intrinsically a set of covectors. In Euclidean coordinates we identify the dual with $\mathbb R^{n}$ through the standard inner product and define the **normal cone** at $x\in K$ by

$$
N_{K}(x)
:=
\left\{
\eta\in\mathbb R^{n}:
\eta^{\mathsf T}(v-x)\leq0
\quad\forall v\in K
\right\}.
$$

The variational inequality

$$
\nabla f(x)^{\mathsf T}(v-x)\geq0
\qquad
\forall v\in K
$$

is equivalent to

$$
-\nabla f(x)\in N_{K}(x).
$$

Or, more compactly,

$$
\boxed{
0\in\nabla f(x)+N_{K}(x).
}
$$

This notation is useful because it separates two ingredients:

- $\nabla f(x)$ describes the objective;
- $N_{K}(x)$ describes the local geometry of the feasible set.

For the scalar interval,

```math
N_{[\ell,r]}(x)
=
\begin{cases}
(-\infty,0], & x=\ell,\\
\{0\}, & \ell<x<r,\\
[0,\infty), & x=r.
\end{cases}
```

Thus the three-case derivative sign rule from Section 3 is exactly a normal-cone inclusion.

## 7. Equality constraints introduce multipliers

Consider first one smooth equality constraint,

```math
\begin{aligned}
\min_{x\in\mathbb R^{n}}\quad & f(x),\\
\text{subject to}\quad & h(x)=0.
\end{aligned}
```

The multiplier condition is easiest to understand as geometry, not as a matrix identity.

### 7.1 Step 1: feasible motion is tangent to the constraint

Suppose $x_{\ast}$ is a feasible point where the constraint surface is locally regular. Let $x(t)$ be any smooth feasible curve through it:

$$
x(0)=x_{\ast},
\qquad
h(x(t))=0.
$$

Differentiate the constraint at $t=0$:

$$
\nabla h(x_{\ast})^{\mathsf T}\dot x(0)=0.
$$

Thus every feasible velocity

$$
d:=\dot x(0)
$$

is tangent to the constraint surface, while $\nabla h(x_{\ast})$ is normal to it.

### 7.2 Step 2: the objective cannot change to first order along a feasible tangent

Because $x_{\ast}$ is a constrained local minimizer, the one-dimensional function

$$
t\mapsto f(x(t))
$$

has a local minimum at $t=0$. Therefore

$$
\frac{\mathrm d}{\mathrm dt}f(x(t))\rvert_{t=0}
=
\nabla f(x_{\ast})^{\mathsf T}d
=
0
$$

for every feasible tangent direction $d$.

So the objective gradient is orthogonal to the whole tangent space. In other words, once movement along the surface has been exhausted, the remaining objective gradient can only point in a normal direction.

### 7.3 Step 3: the constraint gradient supplies that normal direction

For one regular scalar constraint, the normal space is spanned by $\nabla h(x_{\ast})$. Hence there is a scalar $\lambda$ such that

$$
\nabla f(x_{\ast})
+
\lambda\nabla h(x_{\ast})
=
0.
$$

This is the Lagrange-multiplier equation. The multiplier is the coefficient of the normal reaction needed to balance the objective derivative. The gradient of the objective does **not** vanish; only its tangential component must vanish.

With several equality constraints

$$
h(x)=0,
\qquad
h:\mathbb R^{n}\to\mathbb R^{m},
$$

the same picture holds. Their gradients provide several normal directions, and under the usual local regularity assumption they span the normal space. Therefore there is a vector of multipliers

$$
\lambda\in\mathbb R^{m}
$$

such that

$$
\nabla f(x_{\ast})
+
Dh(x_{\ast})^{\mathsf T}\lambda
=
0.
$$

The precise rank and constraint-qualification hypotheses behind this statement are important but not part of the main thread; they are developed in the companion notes.

### 7.4 Step 4: the Lagrangian packages balance and feasibility

Define

$$
\mathcal L(x,\lambda)
:=
f(x)+\lambda^{\mathsf T}h(x).
$$

Then

$$
\nabla_{x}\mathcal L
=
\nabla f+Dh^{\mathsf T}\lambda,
$$

while

$$
\nabla_{\lambda}\mathcal L=h(x).
$$

The first-order conditions can therefore be written compactly as

```math
\begin{aligned}
\nabla_{x}\mathcal L(x_{\ast},\lambda)&=0,\\
h(x_{\ast})&=0.
\end{aligned}
```

The Lagrangian is therefore not introducing a new physical law. It is a device that packages two requirements already visible geometrically:

```text
objective derivative
    + normal reaction from constraints
    = 0

constraint residual
    = 0
```

There is also a useful sensitivity interpretation. If an equality is written with a right-hand side, changing that right-hand side changes the optimum; under suitable differentiability assumptions, the associated multiplier measures the first-order change of the optimal value, up to the sign convention chosen for the residual and Lagrangian. The companion notes derive this and make the sign dependence explicit.

### 7.5 A two-dimensional check

Consider

$$
f(x_{1},x_{2})
=
\frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{2}+2x_{2}^{2},
$$

subject to

$$
x_{1}+x_{2}=1.
$$

The feasible set is a line. Its tangent direction is any multiple of

$$
\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix},
$$

while the constraint normal is

$$
\nabla h
=
\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}.
$$

At the constrained minimizer, the objective gradient must have no component along the tangent, so it must be parallel to this normal. The Lagrangian

$$
\mathcal L(x_{1},x_{2},\lambda)
=
\frac{1}{2}x_{1}^{2}+2x_{2}^{2}
+\lambda(x_{1}+x_{2}-1)
$$

gives

```math
\begin{aligned}
x_{1}+\lambda&=0,\\
4x_{2}+\lambda&=0,\\
x_{1}+x_{2}&=1.
\end{aligned}
```

Solving,

$$
x_{1}=\frac{4}{5},
\qquad
x_{2}=\frac{1}{5},
\qquad
\lambda=-\frac{4}{5}.
$$

The objective gradient is

$$
\nabla f(x_{\ast})
=
\begin{bmatrix}
4/5\\
4/5
\end{bmatrix},
$$

which is exactly normal to the feasible line. The multiplier term cancels that normal gradient, while the tangential derivative was already zero by constrained optimality.

## 8. Equality-constrained quadratics produce KKT systems

Consider the quadratic program

```math
\begin{aligned}
\min_{x}\quad &
\frac{1}{2}x^{\mathsf T}H x-b^{\mathsf T}x,\\
\text{subject to}\quad & Ax=c,
\end{aligned}
```

where

$$
H=H^{\mathsf T}.
$$

The Lagrangian is

$$
\mathcal L(x,\lambda)
=
\frac{1}{2}x^{\mathsf T}H x-b^{\mathsf T}x
+\lambda^{\mathsf T}(Ax-c).
$$

Differentiate with respect to $x$:

$$
Hx-b+A^{\mathsf T}\lambda=0.
$$

Differentiate with respect to $\lambda$:

$$
Ax=c.
$$

Therefore

```math
\boxed{
\begin{bmatrix}
H & A^{\mathsf T}\\
A & 0
\end{bmatrix}
\begin{bmatrix}
x\\
\lambda
\end{bmatrix}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
b\\
c
\end{bmatrix}.
}
```

This is the canonical equality-constrained **KKT system**.

The zero multiplier block is what gives the system its saddle-point structure. [04 · Numerical linear algebra for PDE and optimization systems](04-numerical-linear-algebra.md) explained why such matrices are typically symmetric indefinite rather than SPD, and why that changes the appropriate solver and preconditioner choices.

For uniqueness, it is enough in the standard convex quadratic case that $A$ have full row rank and that $H$ be positive definite on feasible directions,

$$
d^{\mathsf T}Hd>0
\qquad
\text{for every nonzero }d\in\ker A.
$$

Notice the logic: curvature is needed only along directions that preserve the equality constraint.

## 9. Inequality constraints add signs and complementarity

Now fix one common sign convention: equality constraints are written as $h(x)=0$ and inequalities as $g(x)\leq0$. Other texts may reverse the inequality sign, reverse a residual, or place the multiplier term in the Lagrangian with the opposite sign. Those choices change multiplier signs but not the feasible set or the underlying first-order geometry. The companion notes work through these transformations explicitly; here we keep one convention fixed so that the componentwise signs remain readable.

Consider

```math
\begin{aligned}
\min_{x}\quad & f(x),\\
\text{subject to}\quad & h(x)=0,\\
& g(x)\leq0,
\end{aligned}
```

with equality multipliers $\lambda$ and inequality multipliers $\mu$.

Define

$$
\mathcal L(x,\lambda,\mu)
:=
f(x)+\lambda^{\mathsf T}h(x)+\mu^{\mathsf T}g(x).
$$

Under a suitable constraint qualification, a local minimizer satisfies the first-order Karush–Kuhn–Tucker conditions:

```math
\begin{aligned}
\nabla f(x)
+Dh(x)^{\mathsf T}\lambda
+Dg(x)^{\mathsf T}\mu
&=0,
\\
h(x)&=0,
\\
g(x)&\leq0,
\\
\mu&\geq0,
\\
\mu_{i}g_{i}(x)&=0
\quad\text{for every }i.
\end{aligned}
```

The last relation is **complementary slackness**.

A constraint $g_{i}(x)\leq0$ is **active** when

$$
g_{i}(x)=0,
$$

and **inactive** when

$$
g_{i}(x)<0.
$$

Complementarity says

```text
inactive constraint
    g_i(x) < 0
    ⇒ mu_i = 0

positive multiplier
    mu_i > 0
    ⇒ g_i(x) = 0
```

Thus only active inequalities can exert a multiplier force on stationarity.

### 9.1 Why multiplier existence needs regularity

The KKT equations describe the local geometry of a constrained minimizer only when the chosen constraint representation is sufficiently regular. Degenerate constraints can define a perfectly simple feasible set while having gradients that fail to span the relevant normal directions. In that case a minimizer may exist even though no multipliers satisfying the naive KKT equations exist.

For the problems used in the main chapter, it is enough to remember that KKT conditions are stated **under a constraint qualification**. The companion notes explain full-rank equality constraints, common inequality qualifications, and a concrete degenerate example. This separation lets us use KKT conditions correctly here without turning the main path into a survey of nonlinear-programming regularity theory.

## 10. Box constraints are a concrete KKT system

Consider

$$
\ell_{i}\leq x_{i}\leq r_{i}.
$$

Write the lower and upper inequalities as

$$
\ell_{i}-x_{i}\leq0,
\qquad
x_{i}-r_{i}\leq0.
$$

Associate nonnegative multipliers

$$
\mu_{i}^{L}\geq0,
\qquad
\mu_{i}^{U}\geq0.
$$

For a differentiable objective, stationarity becomes

$$
\nabla f(x)
-
\mu^{L}
+
\mu^{U}
=0.
$$

Complementarity is

```math
\begin{aligned}
\mu_{i}^{L}(x_{i}-\ell_{i})&=0,\\
\mu_{i}^{U}(r_{i}-x_{i})&=0.
\end{aligned}
```

The componentwise cases follow immediately.

### 10.1 Inactive coefficient

If

$$
\ell_{i}<x_{i}<r_{i},
$$

both slacks are positive. Complementarity forces

$$
\mu_{i}^{L}=\mu_{i}^{U}=0,
$$

so

$$
(\nabla f(x))_{i}=0.
$$

### 10.2 Lower-active coefficient

If

$$
x_{i}=\ell_{i},
$$

and the upper bound is not simultaneously active, then $\mu_{i}^{U}=0$. Hence

$$
(\nabla f(x))_{i}=\mu_{i}^{L}\geq0.
$$

### 10.3 Upper-active coefficient

If

$$
x_{i}=r_{i},
$$

then $\mu_{i}^{L}=0$ and

$$
(\nabla f(x))_{i}=-\mu_{i}^{U}\leq0.
$$

We have recovered exactly the variational-inequality sign rule from Section 3, now through KKT multipliers.

## 11. A signed box multiplier is often more convenient

Instead of carrying separate lower and upper multipliers, define one signed multiplier

$$
\lambda
:=
-\mu^{L}+\mu^{U}.
$$

Then stationarity is simply

$$
\nabla f(x)+\lambda=0.
$$

Its sign identifies the bound side:

```text
lower active
    lambda_i <= 0

inactive
    lambda_i = 0

upper active
    lambda_i >= 0
```

This signed multiplier is exactly an element of the normal cone:

$$
\lambda\in N_{K}(x).
$$

So the normal-cone, KKT, and componentwise sign descriptions are not competing formulations. They are different ways of expressing the same first-order geometry.

### 11.1 Check the running box example

For

$$
f(x_{1},x_{2})
=
\frac{1}{2}(x_{1}-2)^{2}
+2(x_{2}+1)^{2},
$$

with

$$
0\leq x_{1}\leq1,
\qquad
-\frac{1}{2}\leq x_{2}\leq2,
$$

the unconstrained minimizer $(2,-1)$ lies outside the box. The constrained minimizer is

$$
x_{\ast}
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
-1/2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The gradient there is

$$
\nabla f(x_{\ast})
=
\begin{bmatrix}
-1\\
2
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The first coefficient is upper-active and has a nonpositive gradient. The second is lower-active and has a nonnegative gradient. Neither derivative component vanishes, yet the point satisfies the constrained first-order conditions.

The signed multiplier is

$$
\lambda
=-\nabla f(x_{\ast})
=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
-2
\end{bmatrix},
$$

positive on the upper-active coefficient and negative on the lower-active one.

## 12. Active-set methods exploit a simple hypothetical question

Suppose for a moment that somebody tells us exactly which inequalities are active at the solution.

Then every active inequality can be imposed as an equality, while every inactive inequality has zero multiplier. The original inequality-constrained problem becomes an equality-constrained problem on that guessed active set.

This observation is the basis of **active-set methods**.

For a quadratic program with linear inequalities, a classical working-set method repeatedly:

```text
choose a working set of constraints
        ↓
solve an equality-constrained quadratic subproblem
        ↓
move as far as feasibility permits
        ↓
add a blocking constraint if one is encountered
        ↓
if the step is zero, inspect multipliers
        ↓
drop a constraint with an incompatible multiplier sign
```

The details matter for a production QP solver, but the conceptual point needed later is smaller:

> identifying active inequalities converts them into equalities, so equality-constrained KKT linear algebra becomes the core of the constrained method.

For very large box-constrained problems, changing one working-set index at a time may be inefficient. Primal-dual active-set methods instead update many classifications simultaneously.

## 13. Primal-dual active sets classify a box using both primal and dual information

Return to the box

$$
K
=
\{x:\ell\leq x\leq r\}
$$

and the signed multiplier $\lambda$ satisfying

$$
\nabla f(x)+\lambda=0.
$$

At a solution:

- lower-active means $x_{i}=\ell_{i}$ and $\lambda_{i}\leq0$;
- upper-active means $x_{i}=r_{i}$ and $\lambda_{i}\geq0$;
- inactive means $\ell_{i}<x_{i}<r_{i}$ and $\lambda_{i}=0$.

A useful classification combines the current multiplier with the signed distance to the bounds. Choose $c>0$ and define

```math
\begin{aligned}
\mathcal A_{U}(x,\lambda)
&:=
\{i:\lambda_{i}+c(x_{i}-r_{i})>0\},\\
\mathcal A_{L}(x,\lambda)
&:=
\{i:\lambda_{i}+c(x_{i}-\ell_{i})<0\},\\
\mathcal I(x,\lambda)
&:=
\{1,\ldots,n\}
\setminus
(\mathcal A_{U}\cup\mathcal A_{L}).
\end{aligned}
```

Why do these tests make sense?

At an exact upper-active coefficient,

$$
x_{i}=r_{i},
\qquad
\lambda_{i}>0,
$$

so

$$
\lambda_{i}+c(x_{i}-r_{i})
=
\lambda_{i}>0.
$$

At an exact lower-active coefficient,

$$
x_{i}=\ell_{i},
\qquad
\lambda_{i}<0,
$$

so

$$
\lambda_{i}+c(x_{i}-\ell_{i})
=
\lambda_{i}<0.
$$

For an inactive exact coefficient,

$$
\lambda_{i}=0,
\qquad
\ell_{i}<x_{i}<r_{i},
$$

and neither active inequality is triggered.

Away from the solution, the distance terms discourage declaring a coefficient active merely because its current multiplier has a small noisy sign while the variable lies well inside the box.

## 14. Once the active sets are fixed, PDAS solves an equality problem

A primal-dual active-set iteration can now be written schematically as

```text
current x, lambda
      ↓
classify lower / inactive / upper
      ↓
set x_i = ell_i on lower-active indices
set x_i = r_i   on upper-active indices
set lambda_i = 0 on inactive indices
      ↓
solve stationarity and any equality constraints
for the remaining unknowns
      ↓
reclassify
      ↓
stop when the sets and residuals are stable
```

For the quadratic box problem

$$
f(x)
=
\frac{1}{2}x^{\mathsf T}H x-b^{\mathsf T}x,
$$

stationarity is

$$
Hx-b+\lambda=0.
$$

Suppose the active sets are fixed and let $\mathcal I$ denote the inactive indices. On $\mathcal I$,

$$
\lambda_{\mathcal I}=0.
$$

On the active indices, $x$ is fixed at the appropriate bound. Partitioning the stationarity equation therefore gives the free system

$$
H_{\mathcal I\mathcal I}x_{\mathcal I}
=
b_{\mathcal I}
-
H_{\mathcal I\mathcal A}x_{\mathcal A}.
$$

After solving for the inactive coordinates, the active multipliers are reconstructed from

$$
\lambda_{\mathcal A}
=
b_{\mathcal A}-(Hx)_{\mathcal A}.
$$

So the nonlinear-looking bound problem is reduced, for a fixed classification, to ordinary linear algebra.

If additional equality constraints are present, the inactive variables and equality multipliers are solved together in a reduced KKT system. This is the form that becomes especially important in PDE-constrained optimization.

## 15. Complementarity can be written as one nonsmooth equation

The same box conditions can be compressed into a pointwise algebraic equation. With the signed multiplier convention above and any $c>0$, define

$$
\Phi_{i}(x,\lambda)
:=
\lambda_{i}
-
\min\{0,\lambda_{i}+c(x_{i}-\ell_{i})\}
-
\max\{0,\lambda_{i}+c(x_{i}-r_{i})\}.
$$

Then the box complementarity conditions are equivalent to

$$
\Phi_{i}(x,\lambda)=0
\qquad
\text{for every }i.
$$

Check the three cases.

### 15.1 Lower active

If

$$
x_{i}=\ell_{i},
\qquad
\lambda_{i}<0,
$$

then the minimum term equals $\lambda_{i}$ and the maximum term vanishes, so $\Phi_{i}=0$.

### 15.2 Inactive

If

$$
\ell_{i}<x_{i}<r_{i},
\qquad
\lambda_{i}=0,
$$

then both selected terms vanish and again $\Phi_{i}=0$.

### 15.3 Upper active

If

$$
x_{i}=r_{i},
\qquad
\lambda_{i}>0,
$$

then the maximum term equals $\lambda_{i}$ and the minimum term vanishes.

The min/max functions are not classically differentiable at their switching points. They are, however, **semismooth**: roughly, they retain enough piecewise differentiable structure for a generalized Newton method to make meaningful Newton-like steps.

A semismooth Newton step chooses the appropriate branch of the min/max expressions, linearizes that branch, and solves the resulting linear system. Those branch choices are exactly the active/inactive classifications used by PDAS.

This is the high-level reason for the well-known statement:

> primal-dual active-set methods for box constraints can be interpreted as semismooth Newton methods for a complementarity equation.

A rigorous treatment requires generalized derivatives and semismooth analysis, which is beyond the prerequisite scope of this chapter.

## 16. Projection and PDAS solve the same first-order condition differently

It is useful to compare the two main routes without treating either as universally superior.

Projected gradient uses the fixed-point viewpoint:

```text
current point
    ↓
unconstrained gradient step
    ↓
project back to feasibility
    ↓
repeat
```

PDAS uses the complementarity viewpoint:

```text
current primal and multiplier
    ↓
classify active/inactive
    ↓
solve equality-constrained system for that classification
    ↓
repeat
```

For simple boxes, projection is extremely cheap and preserves feasibility at every iteration. PDAS asks for more coupled linear algebra, but once the correct active set is identified it can behave like a Newton method on the remaining equations.

Both originate from the same variational inequality. They differ in how they exploit it numerically.
