## Purpose

The main [06 · Constrained optimization](06-constrained-optimization.md) chapter keeps a direct path from feasible directions to variational inequalities, KKT conditions, box complementarity, and primal-dual active sets. This companion collects the subtleties that are useful for interpreting those formulas but would interrupt that path: constraint qualifications, sign conventions, second-order constrained conditions, and a small selection of nearby algorithms.

The final sections assess penalty, augmented-Lagrangian, SQP, classical active-set, and semismooth-Newton ideas specifically by their relevance to PDE-constrained optimization. They are included for orientation, not as additional prerequisites.

## Before you start

You should know the main [06 · Constrained optimization](06-constrained-optimization.md) material and the unconstrained Newton/quasi-Newton ideas from [05 · Unconstrained numerical optimization](05-unconstrained-optimization.md).

## What you will be able to do

After these notes, you should be able to:

- explain why multiplier existence depends on the representation and regularity of constraints;
- distinguish tangent cones from linearized constraint cones;
- translate KKT systems between common residual and multiplier sign conventions;
- explain why flipping a PDE residual flips the corresponding adjoint sign but not the underlying optimality condition;
- state the role of the Hessian of the Lagrangian in second-order constrained conditions;
- distinguish a classical working-set QP method from a primal-dual active-set method;
- explain where penalty, augmented-Lagrangian, and SQP methods fit relative to the project's main formulations;
- understand more concretely why PDAS is related to semismooth Newton.

## Roadmap

We begin with regularity because it determines whether the geometric multiplier picture from the main chapter is valid. We then isolate sign conventions, which are mathematically harmless but a frequent source of apparent contradictions between texts and code. Second-order conditions explain why constrained Newton/KKT systems use the Hessian of the Lagrangian on feasible directions. The remaining sections compare nearby numerical methods and identify which ideas are most useful for PDE optimal control.

## 1. Why equality multipliers need a regular constraint surface

Consider

$$
h(x)=0,
\qquad
h:\mathbb R^{n}\to\mathbb R^{m}.
$$

The main chapter used the picture

$$
\text{tangent directions} = \ker Dh(x_{\ast}),
$$

and

$$
\text{normal directions} = \mathrm{range}(Dh(x_{\ast})^{\mathsf T}).
$$

This is justified when the equality constraints are locally regular, for example when

$$
Dh(x_{\ast})
$$

has full row rank. Geometrically, the feasible set then looks locally like a smooth surface of dimension $n-m$. Analytically, the implicit-function theorem supplies local coordinates on that surface.

At a constrained minimum the objective derivative vanishes on its tangent space:

$$
\nabla f(x_{\ast})^{\mathsf T}d=0
\qquad
\forall d\in\ker Dh(x_{\ast}).
$$

The full-rank condition ensures that the orthogonal complement of this tangent space is exactly generated by the constraint gradients, so

$$
\nabla f(x_{\ast}) + Dh(x_{\ast})^{\mathsf T}\lambda = 0
$$

for some multiplier $\lambda$.

### 1.1 A degenerate representation can destroy multiplier existence

Consider

$$
\min_{x\in\mathbb R} x
$$

subject to

$$
x^{2}=0.
$$

The only feasible point is $x=0$, so it is trivially the minimizer. But

$$
f'(0)=1,
\qquad
h'(0)=0.
$$

No scalar $\lambda$ satisfies

$$
1+\lambda h'(0)=0.
$$

The feasible set is not pathological; its **description** is degenerate. This is the simplest reason constraint qualifications matter.

## 2. Inequality constraints need a relation between feasible and linearized directions

For inequalities, the exact tangent cone is determined by actual nearby feasible points. KKT conditions instead use gradients of the active constraints, which define a **linearized cone**.

A constraint qualification ensures that these two local descriptions agree well enough that every objective derivative nonnegative on feasible directions can be represented by nonnegative combinations of active constraint gradients.

There are several standard qualifications. The Manzoni–Quarteroni–Salsa treatment highlights, among others:

- linear independence of active constraint gradients;
- a direction that moves strictly into all active inequalities, a Cottle-type condition;
- Slater-type strict feasibility conditions for appropriate convex or affine constraints.

The exact hierarchy among constraint qualifications is part of nonlinear-programming theory and is not needed for the project. The practical rule is narrower:

> when KKT conditions are used as necessary conditions, check what regularity assumption makes the multiplier representation valid.

### 2.1 A worked example: the feasible set can stay the same while KKT fails

Consider the elementary problem

$$
\min_{x\in\mathbb R} x
\qquad
\text{subject to}
\qquad
x\geq0.
$$

The minimizer is clearly

$$
x_{\ast}=0.
$$

Write the feasible set first as

$$
g(x)\coloneqq -x\leq0.
$$

Using the convention

$$
\mathcal L(x,\mu)=f(x)+\mu g(x),
\qquad
\mu\geq0,
$$

stationarity at $x_{\ast}$ requires

$$
1-\mu=0,
$$

so the KKT multiplier is

$$
\mu=1.
$$

The active constraint gradient is nonzero,

$$
g'(0)=-1,
$$

and its linearization reproduces the actual feasible directions:

$$
g'(0)d=-d\leq0
\quad\Longleftrightarrow\quad
d\geq0.
$$

Now describe **the same feasible set** by the equivalent inequality

$$
\widetilde g(x)\coloneqq -x^{3}\leq0.
$$

The feasible points are still exactly $x\geq0$, and the minimizer is still $x_{\ast}=0$. But

$$
\widetilde g'(0)=0.
$$

The stationarity equation would be

$$
1+\mu\widetilde g'(0)=1=0,
$$

which no multiplier can satisfy. The linearized inequality is now

$$
0\leq0,
$$

so it incorrectly treats every direction as linearly feasible, even though the true tangent cone contains only $d\geq0$.

Nothing about the optimization problem changed; only its algebraic representation changed. The failure is exactly what a constraint qualification is designed to exclude.

For simple box constraints the geometry is explicit and these issues are usually hidden by the componentwise form.

## 3. Sign conventions change multipliers, not the feasible problem

Multiplier signs often appear inconsistent across optimization texts, PDE derivations, and software. In most cases the discrepancy comes from a different residual orientation.

### 3.1 Equality constraints

The equations

$$
h(x)=0
$$

and

$$
-h(x)=0
$$

define exactly the same feasible set.

If

$$
\mathcal L(x,\lambda)=f(x)+\lambda^{\mathsf T}h(x),
$$

stationarity is

$$
\nabla f+Dh^{\mathsf T}\lambda=0.
$$

Using the residual $-h$ with the same plus-sign Lagrangian gives

$$
\nabla f-Dh^{\mathsf T}\widetilde\lambda=0.
$$

The systems coincide after

$$
\widetilde\lambda=-\lambda.
$$

So a multiplier has no convention-independent sign for an equality constraint.

### 3.2 Sensitivity inherits the same sign choice

Consider a right-hand side parameter

$$
h(x)=b
$$

and write

$$
\mathcal L(x,\lambda) = f(x)+\lambda^{\mathsf T}(h(x)-b).
$$

Suppose the minimizer $x_{\ast}(b)$ and optimal value

$$
V(b)\coloneqq f(x_{\ast}(b))
$$

are differentiable. Differentiate feasibility:

$$
Dh(x_{\ast})\frac{\partial x_{\ast}}{\partial b}=I.
$$

Stationarity gives

$$
\nabla f(x_{\ast})=-Dh(x_{\ast})^{\mathsf T}\lambda.
$$

Hence

$$
\frac{\partial V}{\partial b}
= \nabla f(x_{\ast})^{\mathsf T}
  \frac{\partial x_{\ast}}{\partial b} = -\lambda.
$$

Had we written the multiplier term as $\lambda^{\mathsf T}(b-h(x))$, the multiplier would have the opposite sign and the sensitivity formula would read $\partial V/\partial b=\lambda$. The sensitivity interpretation is real; its sign is tied to the residual convention.

### 3.3 Inequality orientation and multiplier signs

Suppose a constraint is written as

$$
g(x)\leq0.
$$

A common convention is

$$
\mathcal L(x,\mu) = f(x)+\mu^{\mathsf T}g(x),
\qquad
\mu\geq0.
$$

Now define

$$
\widetilde g(x)\coloneqq -g(x)\geq0.
$$

One can write the same KKT geometry as

$$
\mathcal L(x,\widetilde\mu) =
f(x)-\widetilde\mu^{\mathsf T}\widetilde g(x),
\qquad
\widetilde\mu\geq0.
$$

The two expressions are identical when $\widetilde\mu=\mu$ because $-\widetilde g=g$. What must be changed together is the inequality orientation **and** the sign with which its multiplier enters the Lagrangian.

This is why memorizing “the multiplier is positive” without also remembering the residual convention is dangerous.

### 3.4 Normal-cone sign conventions

The main chapter defines

$$
N_{K}(x)\coloneqq\lbrace\eta:\eta^{\mathsf T}(v-x)\leq0\ \forall v\in K\rbrace,
$$

so constrained stationarity is

$$
0\in\nabla f(x)+N_{K}(x).
$$

Some sources use the opposite inequality in the definition of the normal cone. Their stationarity inclusion then carries the opposite sign. Again, the geometry is unchanged once the pair of conventions is transformed consistently.

### 3.5 PDE residuals and adjoint signs

The same issue appears in PDE-constrained optimization. Suppose the PDE is encoded as

$$
E(y,u)=0
$$

and the Lagrangian convention is

$$
\mathcal L(y,u,p) = J(y,u)-\langle p,E(y,u)\rangle.
$$

If instead the application defines

$$
\widetilde E\coloneqq -E,
$$

then the same Lagrangian value is obtained by replacing

$$
\widetilde p\coloneqq -p.
$$

State feasibility is identical; the adjoint variable changes sign because it is the multiplier attached to the chosen residual orientation. This is exactly why a project-wide Lagrangian sign convention is useful.

## 4. Second-order conditions use the Hessian of the Lagrangian

For an unconstrained stationary point, second-order conditions use

$$
\nabla^{2}f(x_{\ast}).
$$

With equality constraints, only tangent directions are feasible to first order, and the relevant curvature object becomes the Hessian of the Lagrangian,

$$
\nabla_{xx}^{2}\mathcal L(x_{\ast},\lambda_{\ast}).
$$

For a regular equality-constrained problem, a standard second-order necessary condition is

$$
d^{\mathsf T}
\nabla_{xx}^{2}\mathcal L(x_{\ast},\lambda_{\ast})
d
\geq0
$$

for every tangent direction

$$
Dh(x_{\ast})d=0.
$$

A strict positivity condition on every nonzero tangent direction is a standard local sufficient condition.

This explains a feature that otherwise looks mysterious in Newton–KKT and SQP methods: the upper-left block is the Hessian of the **Lagrangian**, not merely the Hessian of the objective.

### 4.1 Inequalities and the critical cone

For inequalities, tangent directions must also respect active constraints. Moreover, some active constraints have zero multipliers, so first-order information may still allow motion along them. The appropriate second-order test is therefore restricted to a **critical cone** that combines:

- equality-tangent directions;
- active inequalities with positive multipliers treated as equalities;
- active inequalities with zero multipliers allowing only first-order feasible motion.

The Manzoni–Quarteroni–Salsa text develops this construction in Section 2.3.3. It is important for full constrained Newton theory, but not necessary for understanding box PDAS at the level used by the project.

## 5. Classical active-set QP and PDAS are related but not identical

For a convex quadratic program with linear inequalities, a classical active-set method maintains a **working set** of constraints treated as equalities. Given the current working set it solves an equality-constrained QP, then either:

- moves until a new blocking constraint becomes active; or
- if no feasible descent step remains, inspects multiplier signs and releases an inconsistent active constraint.

The method therefore changes the working set incrementally. The Manzoni–Quarteroni–Salsa text notes that for large box-constrained problems this can be slow because only one constraint may be added or removed at a time.

PDAS uses the same fundamental observation — a known active set turns inequalities into equalities — but classifies many components simultaneously from a primal-dual test. That is much closer to the structure of large discretized control problems, where thousands of control degrees of freedom may switch status together.

The NMOPT course makes this concrete: after the active control degrees of freedom are identified, they are imposed as affine equalities and the remaining KKT system is linear.

## 6. Penalty and augmented-Lagrangian methods: useful orientation, not prerequisites

The Manzoni–Quarteroni–Salsa optimization chapter also presents penalty and augmented-Lagrangian methods. They are important general tools, but the current project background does not need them in the main path.

### 6.1 Quadratic penalties trade feasibility for conditioning

For schematic constraints

$$
h(x)=0,
$$

a quadratic penalty replaces the constrained problem by

$$
\min_{x}
\left[
 f(x) + \frac{\mu}{2}\lVert h(x)\rVert^{2}
\right].
$$

As $\mu$ grows, constraint violations become more expensive and minimizers of the penalized problem can approach the constrained solution under suitable assumptions.

The drawback is already visible in the Hessian: large $\mu$ creates very stiff curvature in directions that change the constraints. Enforcing feasibility more strongly can therefore make the unconstrained subproblem increasingly ill-conditioned.

This tension is particularly relevant for PDE problems, where the underlying linear algebra is already expensive.

### 6.2 Augmented Lagrangians keep multiplier information

An augmented Lagrangian combines multiplier and penalty terms. For equality constraints one schematic form is

$$
\mathcal L_{A}(x,\lambda,\mu) = f(x) +
\lambda^{\mathsf T}h(x) + \frac{\mu}{2}\lVert h(x)\rVert^{2},
$$

with signs adapted to the chosen convention.

Rather than relying only on an ever larger $\mu$, the method also updates $\lambda$ using the observed constraint residual. The multiplier carries an estimate of the normal reaction at the solution; the penalty term stabilizes the intermediate subproblems.

This family is worth recognizing because it is a standard alternative when one does not want to solve a monolithic KKT system directly. It is not needed to understand `nmopt`'s present reduced/KKT/PDAS formulations.

## 7. SQP is constrained Newton expressed as a sequence of QPs

Sequential quadratic programming is more directly connected to the KKT material.

For a nonlinear equality-constrained problem, write the KKT residual schematically as

$$
F(x,\lambda) = \begin{bmatrix}
\nabla_{x}\mathcal L(x,\lambda)\\
h(x)
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

Applying Newton's method to

$$
F(x,\lambda)=0
$$

produces a linear system with the saddle-point matrix

$$
\begin{bmatrix}
\nabla_{xx}^{2}\mathcal L & Dh^{\mathsf T}\\
Dh & 0
\end{bmatrix}.
$$

The same step can be interpreted as the solution of a quadratic model of the objective/Lagrangian subject to a linearization of the constraints. That is the basic SQP idea.

For general nonlinear programs, each iteration therefore solves a QP, updates multipliers, and uses line-search or trust-region globalization. A quasi-Newton approximation such as BFGS may replace the exact Lagrangian Hessian.

This is highly relevant conceptually to nonlinear PDE optimal control because it combines three themes already present in the background chapters:

```text
Newton linearization
        +
KKT saddle-point systems
        +
quadratic constrained subproblems
```

It is nevertheless not a prerequisite for the current project manual, which can introduce nonlinear optimality systems without requiring a full SQP implementation.

## 8. PDAS as a semismooth Newton method

For a scalar box

$$
\ell\leq u\leq r
$$

and the signed multiplier convention from the main chapter, complementarity can be encoded by the nonsmooth equation

$$
F(u,\lambda)\coloneqq \lambda -
\min\lbrace 0,\lambda+c(u-\ell)\rbrace -
\max\lbrace 0,\lambda+c(u-r)\rbrace = 0,
$$

with $c\gt0$.

Check the three exact cases.

If $u=\ell$ and $\lambda\lt0$, then

$$
\lambda+c(u-\ell)=\lambda\lt0,
$$

while the upper expression remains nonpositive. The equation reduces correctly to zero.

If $\ell\lt u\lt r$ and $\lambda=0$, both active pieces vanish and again $F=0$.

If $u=r$ and $\lambda\gt0$, the upper max returns $\lambda$ while the lower min vanishes, again giving $F=0$.

The functions $\min$ and $\max$ are not classically differentiable at their switching points, but they are piecewise linear and admit generalized derivatives. A semismooth Newton step chooses the derivative corresponding to the current active/inactive classification. Once that classification is fixed, the resulting Newton system is exactly the equality-constrained linear system solved by PDAS.

This is the mathematical reason the two descriptions are so closely linked:

```text
PDAS viewpoint
    guess switching sets and solve the corresponding equality problem

semismooth Newton viewpoint
    linearize a nonsmooth complementarity equation using its current branch
```

The NMOPT course and the Manzoni–Quarteroni–Salsa treatment both use this relation to connect box complementarity with active-set algorithms.

## 9. Methods especially relevant to PDE-constrained optimization

Among the nearby constrained methods, **PDAS and its semismooth-Newton interpretation** are the most directly connected to the box-constrained optimality systems developed later. They preserve the KKT viewpoint and reduce each active-set guess to an equality-constrained problem, so the saddle-point linear algebra from the previous chapters remains visible.

**SQP** is also worth understanding at a conceptual level because it is Newton's method applied to nonlinear KKT equations, or equivalently a sequence of quadratic models with linearized constraints. That viewpoint becomes useful whenever a nonlinear PDE or nonlinear all-at-once optimality system is considered, even if a general-purpose SQP implementation is outside the project.

**Augmented-Lagrangian methods** provide a complementary perspective: multiplier estimates are updated explicitly while moderate penalty terms enforce feasibility, avoiding some of the extreme conditioning caused by a pure penalty method. Pure penalties are still useful pedagogically because they make the feasibility-versus-conditioning tradeoff unmistakable. Classical working-set QP methods, meanwhile, are the finite-dimensional ancestor of the active-set logic used by PDAS.

Interior-point methods are an important general alternative, but the sources used here discuss them only peripherally and the project does not rely on their machinery. A full treatment would broaden the chapter into general nonlinear programming without improving the background needed for the present PDE-control formulations.
